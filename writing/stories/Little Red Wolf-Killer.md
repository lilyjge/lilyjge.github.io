Little Red Wolf-Killer  
A lone girl in a red hooded cloak walked the forest under the light of the rising full moon. Her short flaming red hair hung around her shoulders, and her innocent smile was of a child’s, though she was nearly an adult now. She held a lantern in one hand with a basket of pastries hooked on an arm. A delicious smell drifted from the basket, attracting all the wrong sorts of attention.   
	It was dangerous to walk alone at night through the forest, everyone had said. She wasn’t afraid, though. She had walked this path from her house to her Grandmother's cottage many times, day and night, and knew the twists and bends like the back of her hand. This night was no different.   
	The girl spotted a growth of wildflowers by the path, the earliest of the season. Why, Grandmother would love these, she thought as she bent down to pick some, setting her basket and lantern on a stump. A wild variety of blossoms bloomed, and the girl took her time selecting the prettiest ones.   
	Across the path and hidden in the trees, amber eyes flashed. A silver wolf hid in the shadows, drawn to the bright light and human scent. Its prey was oblivious to it, with its back turned. The wolf leaped forward soundlessly, expecting an easy kill.   
	The girl whirled around, her cloak fluttering and her hood falling off. Her silver dagger, now unsheathed, was already in her hand as she jumped to the side and darted behind the wolf. The flowers fell to the ground in a heap. The wolf turned, growled, and struck again.   
	A flash of red as the girl dodged, and another of silver as she attacked. The wolf was on the ground, howling in pain. The dagger pierced the wolf’s side and blood leaked onto its fur, staining it red, as the girl stepped back, quietly observing. It twitched, and let out one last anguished howl before going still.  
	The girl tugged her hood back on as she retrieved her dagger. From a distance, a choir of howls pierced the night air. Judging from the sound, they would be there soon, and there was no need to go to them. She wiped the dagger on her cloak and sheathed it as she bent to recollect her flowers.  
	“Barbarians,” she muttered, replacing the flowers the wolf had trampled. Satisfied, she put the bunch into her basket and looked up. The moon was not at its peak, and she might still reach Grandmother’s house before midnight.   
	 A bird chirped in the distance as the girl walked on. There was another round of howls, closer this time.   
	The path opened into a clearing, with a large rock in the middle, perhaps so that a traveller may rest. She decided to sit for a while to rest.  
	She placed her basket beside her and sat down on the large rock. Her eyes closed and she sat there for a moment, listening to a bird’s song. Soon, it fell silent and the girl heard a growl. She opened her eyes.  
	Wolves circled her around the clearing, six in all. She locked eyes with the alpha, a slightly bigger beast than the others with fur that looked almost black. Its eyes were a pale green and the girl stared it down, gradually standing up and taking out her dagger. With a yowl, the wolves charged.  
	They were a storm of fur and claws. The girl spun around, slicing everything in her path. A paw clawed at her, and she brought her dagger upon an exposed hindleg. There was a whine of pain, and she knew it had connected. The wolves teared at her cloak, but it was made of a special material with metal weaved in it, impenetrable to claws.   
	One of the wolves bit at her arm, tearing her sleeve. She managed to stab it in the neck, and it dropped with a cry. The remaining five howled and came at her with even more ferocity.   
	She slashed at the animals, leaving a long cut down the side of one and cutting the leg of another. Her sleeves were torn and scratches ran down her arms, but she kept fighting. One flew at her, but she ducked and thrusted her dagger into its exposed belly. Another one down.  
	The wolves seemed to realise that her neck was unprotected, and leaped at it. However, the girl was prepared, and as one tried to bite her, she slit its neck. Her calf burned, and she whirled around and cut down another wolf that had just left a long cut down the back of her right leg.   
	Two wolves remained, circling her and growling. One of them was the alpha, with a cut on its face, while the other had a gash down its side and a scar over one eye. The girl stood her ground, panting heavily. It wouldn’t be long now.  
	She and the wolves sprang at the same time. With a yell, she brought her dagger down to the wolf with one eye. It dodged, but she managed to slice its ear and land a blow to its leg. The alpha pounced and she sidestepped just in time for its claw to rake against her side. She decided to focus on the half-blind one first, since it was obviously weaker, and jumped at it.  
	The wolf backed away in alarm, unused to prey not running away. The girl circled from behind it to its blind side, and struck. It backed away to avoid a killing blow, but the dagger pierced its front leg and it wouldn’t be able to fight anytime soon.   
	She turned to face the alpha as it sneered. It pounced first, aiming low at her calves. She swerved around, but it followed her, clawing at her. She was careful not to let it land a blow, especially on her exposed skin. It stood up as it prepared to strike, but the girl was faster, and her dagger was buried in its chest.  
	For a moment, the beast seemed surprised. Its brilliantly green eyes widened, then it slumped forward, and its head hung. The girl pulled out her dagger and the body fell to the floor with a thump. Its form changed, shrinking until it was the shape of a dead boy, no older than the girl herself.   
	The girl would have smiled, but she frowned, for that she recognized the boy. He was at the last convention of the Hunter’s Guild, a new apprentice, trained by the same man she herself was trained by. Disgusted, she stepped over the body to check on the wounded wolf.   
	It was still alive, and was now tenderly licking its wounds. It didn’t seem to have any of the ferocity as before, as the girl almost pitied it. Wolves were not aggressive creatures by nature, and the nature of that one was certainly showing through. It made no attempt to attack her.   
	“Run away, as far as you can,” the girl said. Of course, she didn’t expect it to understand her, and she laughed at her own silliness.   
	The wolf tilted its head curiously, almost in a friendly manner. The girl smiled, and turned away. Its wounds were not deep, and though it would have trouble hunting for a while, it would survive.   
	She sheathed her dagger and picked up her basket. The moon was almost at its full height now, but she was close to her grandmother’s house. The sleeves of her shirt were torn and so were the legs of her pants. Scratches and bite marks ran down her arms and calves, but none was too deep and could wait until she reached the house.   
	The girl started on the path again. The salty tang of blood hung in the air, and she felt rather hot, despite the cool spring air. The familiar path twisted, and she stood in front of her grandmother’s house.  
	The familiar crest-fallen feeling greeted her. She knew what it was like these days, and she didn’t know what she expected. It wasn’t her first time here, but it was still disappointing to her to see the place, a bitter pain that hurt her more than any scratch.   
	Her grandmother’s lovely cottage had once been such a beautiful place, and that was the image she had hoped against hope she would see every time she walked the path. Yet there it was. The garden, once filled with flowers, was now overgrown with weeds that seemed even more vicious than the last times she was here. Vines crept over the walls and the roof, nature attempting to reclaim the place, despite the girl’s best efforts. The colour of the walls and the roof, once bright and cheery, now seemed faded and melancholy. It was all she could do to keep the place from falling to ruins, but there it stood, a sad mockery to its former glory.   
	She took a deep breath and approached the door. The little cobblestone pass had moss growing in the cracks, and she winced as a weed brushed a scratch on her leg. Surprisingly, the door was already open when she reached it. The girl wasn’t overly alarmed as there were no valuables left, but she hadn’t expected visitors. The door squeaked as it opened, a sad, choking sound. She held out her lantern cautiously.   
	It was almost exactly as she had left it last time. The old fireplace, the armchair where her grandmother used to knit, the small kitchen, the doors leading to the bedrooms and the washroom, and the dining table all neatly in place and covered with a thin layer of dust. A heavy man sat in a chair by the table, dozing, his musket leaning against a wall.   
	The girl approached the man and set her lantern and basket on the table. He was a rough man with an unruly beard, broad shouldered and large, about the age her parents would’ve been if they lived. He blinked in the light of the lantern, yawned, and stretched. The girl turned away to light the fireplace, repulsed by the sight of him.   
	“Ah, Red\!” the man exclaimed in a low and gruff voice. The girl ignored him and continued on the fire. His gaze bored into the back of her head, but she waited until the fire was burning before she responded.   
	“What brings you here, Hunter?”  
	She had thought it funny, at first. A hunter named Hunter. All these years ago, yet it could have been yesterday.  
	“What, I can’t visit my favorite apprentice?” the man chuckled. Red opened a window to let in some fresh air and wiped the dust off the fireplace with a rag she’d found, not acknowledging the man.  
	“You were such a cute little thing back then,” Hunter cooed. Red closed her eyes and forced herself to exhale.  
	“You disgust me,” she said coldly. Maybe it was retribution for her own coolness, but Hunter did not respond or appear to have heard.  
	“Tiny little girl, lugging an axe half her own size,” he continued. He didn’t need to, for Red could see the scene as clear as day playing out before her.  
	*She had only been eight. The wolf had fled when she came into her grandmother’s room with the heavy axe, but not before she had managed to land two blows in the blind darkness. She had stumbled out of the cottage, and followed the wolf’s trail of blood, but it soon stopped. So she wandered around aimlessly, hauling the axe and screaming for the wolf to come out, to face her, all the way through night.*  
	“You were so good back then, not the ungrateful little wrench you are now,” Hunter sighed, but Red could not care less about his opinion.   
	*He had found her near dawn, still dazed from the anger and shock. He showed her to his trap, and the wolf stuck in it, bleeding exactly where she had struck it. She had been prepared to kill it, but he had told her to wait for the sun to rise. And when it did, it was not a wolf in the net, but a young man.*   
	“I am not ungrateful. I fully appreciate what you did for me,” she said in an attempt to remain civil. The man snorted.   
	*He had told her that the man was a werewolf, the most detestable of monsters. Only silver could kill it, and he had offered her a beautiful silver dagger and shown her exactly where to strike. She had done it.*  
	“You never come to visit me, not even at the last convention, with me right next door\!” Hunter said accusingly. She gripped the rag tight and laughed mirthlessly.  
	*She had not noticed that she was hungry or that she had not slept. The hunter later told her that she had fainted, and that he had taken her back to his cabin. When she woke, he had given her some food and water and asked her about what had happened. She had told him, almost crying. He had helped her build a grave for her grandmother in the backyard of the cottage. It had been past noon when they finished, and they had lunch. He had offered to teach her how to kill wolves and werewolves, but she had declined. Wolves aren’t naturally aggressive creatures, she had been taught. Werewolves might be different, but maybe it was only that one, she had thought. And so, the hunter escorted her back to her village, only to find that-*  
	“My whole village was slaughtered,” Red’s voice was eerily calm as she turned to face her mentor. “Revenge from the wolves for my first kill. They couldn’t kill me, because I was with you. You could have told me. You could have saved them. You could have killed the werewolf yourself and none of them would have died and I would still have my family.”   
	“Now, looky here, Red,” he dared to smile at her. “You’re being a little harsh, aren’t ya? I didn’t exactly plan for it to happen. You had potential, and it would be a terrible pity if you didn’t use it. It’s not like I arranged for the wolves to attack.”  
	Red found it miraculous that she did not kill him right then and there. “Do you have a reason to be here, Hunter? Did the Council send you?”  
	“Well, now you mentioned it, my current apprentice is on his first hunt somewhere around here, and the council has a message,” Hunter stood up and walked to Red’s side. She moved away from him.  
	“Your apprentice is dead. Turned into a werewolf and I killed him. What does the Council want?” Red said without looking at him. The man sighed.   
	“Tsk, tsk. I’ve almost forgotten how harsh you are. The Council said that they will consider your ideas and you are to attend to them next quarter moon. The ideas you came up with are very interesting, I gotta say, though a bit crazy. Werewolves controlling normal wolves, Red? Werewolves being able to control when they turn? And the subtle suggestion that a werewolf had infiltrated the Council was quite intriguing. Would you care to explain? Those documents you sent were vague.” He followed her to the shelf she dusted, so she moved to the other side of the room.   
	“Normal wolves don’t slaughter an entire village in revenge for one. They don’t guard a town and kill everyone that tries to settle there and they don’t keep terrorising one person over and over for almost a decade. They don’t suddenly stop attacking after you kill the werewolf,” she snapped. Her temper rose as he again followed her.   
	“People call you the wolf-killer, you know. How many have you killed by now, Red?” he mused, ignoring her. Red did a mental tally as she retaliated.   
	“Twenty-one werewolves and countless wolves. They all attacked me first, all in this forest. It can’t be a coincidence, those numbers are far higher than the average.” She gave up on the rag and swept with a broom. Hunter followed her like an annoying pest.  
	“Have you considered not wearing a bright red cloak and having a basket of excellent pastries?” The corner of his mouth twitched. “Can I have one?”  
	“Help yourself,” Red said. It wasn’t like her grandmother was actually going to eat them. She usually sat by her grave and pretended they were having tea together while eating by herself and occasionally feeding a squirrel. At least it would get him to stop following her. “Just don’t touch the flowers. Shouldn’t you get ready to leave?”  
	Hunter sat down and stuffed a treat in his mouth, ignoring her. She lost her appetite just by looking at the disgusting way he ate, so she turned away. Without his annoyance, she allowed herself a smile that the Council had finally acknowledged her.   
	“A bit too sweet,” she heard him say, muffled by chewing. “But delicious otherwise.”  
	“You know,” he said after swallowing. “Most of us don’t live that long.”  
	“The average werewolf hunter hunts for less than a decade before they are inevitably scratched or bitten,” Red recited automatically. She paused. “My decade is almost up, yet you have hunted for more than two.”  
	“I must admit that I am very talented,” the man said. She snorted and fell silent again.   
	“You have not told me your reasoning for suspecting that the Council has been infiltrated,” he said after a moment. To her indignation, he started toward her again.   
	“Lack of experimentation. Witches and shapeshifters and all the other ones are being studied and experimented on, yet not werewolves, far easier to capture than those others. If they bothered, my theories could be proved already,” Red took a deep breath. “And the rumours of a peace treaty. No hint of a treaty has been talked of for any other creature. My suggestions will set it back, of course, but I think the Council should know what they’re capable of before agreeing to anything.”  
	“Clever, Red. You’d make a great werewolf,” he cackled.   
	Startled, she turned around to face him, but he looked out the window, apparently not noticing. Taking the chance, she quietly moved away.   
	“I’ve wondered, what would it be like if the roles were reversed? If werewolves hunted humans because they were weak. Almost sounds like the natural order of the world, doesn’t it?” he pondered. He turned to her, his expression unreadable in the dim firelight.   
	“Sounds like you’re growing soft,” Red said. He chuckled darkly.   
	“Would you like to be a werewolf, Red? To be stronger and faster?” he breathed, the words barely audible from across the room. Her hand went to her dagger.   
	“In a world where they don’t slaughter villages, maybe. But if they didn’t, we wouldn’t hunt them,” she said carefully.   
	“I’ll take that as a no,” Hunter sighed. “Pity, Red. You were always my favorite. And you tried so hard, but you never could beat me.”  
His form changed. Fur grew from his body as he grew taller and bigger, his clothes ripping and falling. She didn’t wait for him to complete, and dashed forward, dagger in hand.  
He dodged easily, and the floor hissed as Red whirled around. The beast was the largest wolf she had ever seen, growling at her with its face twisted almost in a smirk. She charged.   
Her dagger sliced through the air, faster than her own eyes could perceive. Yet somehow, he was behind her, claws tearing at her hair and back, only blocked by her armour. She bit her tongue as she pulled away and stabbed at him.   
He was fast, too fast. Worse, he knew her every move, because he had taught her. But he did not strike the killing blow. One last fight with his favorite apprentice, Red thought bitterly.   
They stood facing each other in the living room, the furniture scattered and knocked over. She breathed heavily, but he did not seem tired in the least. Red normally didn’t like talking during a fight, but she had to know something.  
“It was you all along. You killed my grandmother. That first werewolf, the boy, it wasn’t him. The werewolves in this forest, your rivals, you sent them here to use me to kill them,” she said, an eerie calm settling over her. She wanted to ask about the specifics, if he had sent the wolves that destroyed her family and why, but she doubted he could answer her in this form.   
The wolf’s lips curled and he dipped his head in an unmistakable nod. It was all the confirmation she needed, and with a scream of fury, she lunged.   
His claws tore at her cloak, her arms, her legs, her body. She was vaguely aware of a burning sensation in her calf, but she kept tearing and stabbing and slashing. There was a sharp pain in her hand, and she was thrown across the room, hitting the opposite wall roughly and sliding down.  
	Pain wrecked through her body and her head pounded. Her vision swam, and she saw through the stars that her dagger lay a little bit off. She grabbed it and gripped the hilt tightly.  
	The wolf approached her. It had scratched down her side and blood in its matted fur, but it was still in far better condition than Red was. With her last remaining strength, she threw her dagger. He shifted and it sailed past him, hitting a wall. The wolf made a noise that resembled laughter.  
	She stared blankly as he stalked towards her. His movement was slowing, growing more and more clumsy, yet she wasn’t touching him. He swayed, stumbled, and finally, collapsed.  
	Red waited for a full moment before she laughed. Her voice rasped, and she laughed until she was out of breath. Slowly, inching up the wall, she got to her feet.  
	He sneered, tried to get up, and fell down again. She kicked him in the stomach.  
	“You shouldn’t accept food from strangers, or from people you try to kill,” she said, breathing heavily as she laughed in triumph. His eyes widened and he howled furiously.   
	The poison wasn’t enough to kill him, though she had poured in the whole bottle. Well, he wasn’t going anywhere, and Red stumbled to retrieve her dagger, a slow and painful process. When she got back, he was no longer whimpering, but trying to crawl away. She plunged the dagger down. He let out one last painful howl and stretched out a paw. He pointed to her calf, with one last smirk, his body fell still and turned back into a human.  
	Red looked down. Her right calf was covered in blood from a long cut that had not been there before. Moonlight streamed through the window, and Red fell, convulsing.   
	In the cottage, a lone wolf howled.
