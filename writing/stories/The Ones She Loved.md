THE ONES SHE LOVED  

A young woman, or at least a woman that appeared young, parked her car in front of the grand mansion on the cliff by the sea. She took her suitcase from the trunk and approached the house.   
It was an old place that had been left to her by her teacher and one that she had not visited in years, despite spending her childhood there. Sometimes she liked its melancholy and foreboding aura, sometimes she did not.   
Moonlight shone brightly, reflecting off the dark sea. The woman was ethereally beautiful, with skin pale as a ghost’s and light blonde hair that almost gleamed white. The house must have been built before she was even born, but somehow she fit right in.   
The door gave a shrill creak as she unlocked and opened it.   
“Ah,” she whispered. “Home, sweet home.”  
The layers of dust could be dealt with tomorrow. She had a long day, so as soon as she made her room acceptable for a good night’s rest, she went to sleep.   
\~  
The mansion was restored to its past glory, brilliant golden lights shining above the ballroom. Music played and people in the most exquisite clothes danced and laughed. Everything was lively and bright.   
A man sat by the grand piano, weaving a heavenly melody. He was enraptured in his tune, like it was his whole soul and being. Caught in his own world, oblivious to all that was around him as his fingers danced on the keyboard.   
His hair was dark, as were his eyes. He possessed a cool elegance from his countenance and a divine charm from the light in his eyes that could only be created by the joy of art.   
He was breathtaking perfection.   
“Lovely,” the young woman murmured. “So, so, lovely.”  
She had been watching him for a very long time. Finally, she was ready to speak to him, and a thrill rushed over her at the prospect. He aroused such inexplicable feelings in her, wondrous but despicable that he should not be hers alone to admire.   
But before she could go, someone else was already there.   
It was a disgusting little thing, wearing what could only be described as rags. Her face was so plain that it was painful to look at. Yet when he saw her, it was with fierce and burning passion that a smile spread on his features. He gazed at her as if she were the only light in the darkness, like she was his saviour and muse, and with absolute adoration.   
They conversed, and the creature laughed. Then he held out his hand, and she had the audacity to take it, to dance with him.   
The agony of a thousand daggers stabbed into her heart was not as terrible as this. She had never felt hatred this intense, that he should look upon anyone that was not her in such a way. It was hard to breathe when that vile thing dared to touch him so affectionately.   
The air around her froze. Softly, she snapped her fingers.   
The creature convulsed and fell, but the man caught her. His eyes narrowed, worry creeping over his face. There was nothing he could do as the creature went limp.   
For a moment, all was serene.   
Then, someone screamed. People rushed past each other, and she lost sight of the man. When she saw him next, he had the creature in his arms and tears streaking down his cheeks. He was calling for help, which was quite idiotic, since the thing was clearly dead as can be.   
She was impatient for the people to leave, because she wanted to have him alone. He was sobbing, and she longed to comfort him, to have him look at her.   
Finally, the doctors and guests left. He went down to the sea, walking slowly.   
The woman hesitated briefly, excitement building up once more. She ran along the beach until she caught up to him.   
“Hello. Beautiful night, isn’t it?”  
The man nodded absently. “I suppose.”  
“I’m Celeste,” she said. “We met at the party.”  
He did not reply, much to her annoyance. They walked together for some time, under the romantic moonlight to the sound of the crashing waves. She enjoyed his company, and found the silence almost comfortable, though she did wish he would proclaim his love for her. But when he spoke, it was not the words she wanted.   
“I apologize,” he said politely. “But could you leave me? I’ve had rather an eventful evening.”  
The woman, Celeste, stopped and stared. Her heart pounded too loudly for head to function.   
“I-I don’t understand,” she said.   
He sighed, his voice weary. “The one I loved died tonight.”  
“I know, I saw,” she said. “But she’s dead now. She isn’t coming back.”  
So he could love her now. He could hold her like he did with that thing and look at her instead.   
Instead, his expression twisted with hurt and sadness. He took a step backwards.   
“Yes,” he breathed. “She’s gone.”  
Celeste reached out a hand, wanting to comfort him, to show him that the thing didn’t matter anymore, now she was here. She needed him with desperation that could drive her mad, but he pushed her away.   
Abruptly, he strode away. So she stood there, her pride wounded, and her heart shattered. She craved to chase after him, to demand his love, but she knew that if she were to go right now, he would never speak to her again, much less love her.   
She watched his figure walk along the seaside, until she could no longer bear the torment. Fleeing back to the house, she stomped to her room and cried into her pillow all night, wallowing in her own misery and tears like never before.   
When she woke the next morning, it was with an aching emptiness inside, but she comforted herself with the knowledge that surely he was, after all, not unreasonable. Soon he would have forgotten all about the thing, and embrace her with open arms.   
Slightly cheered, Celeste went on with her day. It was a good morning, until she saw from her window several men carrying a stretcher from the sea, and the body lying on it.   
She ran from the house. Down, down, down, too many stairs to the road. She rushed in front of the men with the stretcher, and saw with her very own eyes, the corpse of the man she loved.   
Celeste sank to her knees, which hit the ground with pain that didn’t come close to what she felt. Tears streamed down her face and she wept, of anguish and despair. She prayed she could weep out all her sorrows and woes, for it to all be an awful dream, and her prayer went unanswered.   
“I heard he threw himself into the sea after his lover died,” one of the men muttered to another.   
“Pity, he was so young, and a good musician too,” said another.   
“Wait,” said a third. “His lover died? Then who is this?”  
Even when all was dead and gone, her suffering would remain. This she was sure of. The world had ended, so there was no more need for the sun nor the moon. Relinquish everything to the void, and return to the darkness where all once originated.   
\~  
The woman woke with a start and the lament of an old wound reopened.   
She could still hear the music of that night playing. If only he could remain that way, alive, beautiful, and mesmerized and ecstatic in his music.   
This was why she did not return here often. It brought back too many memories.   
There was no more chance of sleep, so she made her way to the library.   
Like the rest of the house, it was covered in dust, having been untouched for years. But on the writing desk, a few books lay where they had been left, along with some old drawings done by a child. If he were alive, he must have grown up a long time ago.   
The woman smiled slightly and sat. She had read those books, fairy tales, many times, since the child had liked them very much. She went through the drawings and stopped at one.   
There were four stick figures, not drawn very well. An old woman, with ‘Grandma’ scrawled on top. A slightly taller woman, with ‘Mama’. A girl, ‘Anne’. And finally, a little boy.   
‘Peter.’  
The woman leaned back, closed her eyes, and allowed memories to wash over her anew.   
\~  
Celeste didn’t return to the town until everyone that knew him was dead, and only to the summon of Master, whose every word she obeyed, and who she reluctantly admitted she had missed dearly.   
Master appeared as a kind and elderly woman. She did not come out to greet Celeste, perhaps to punish her for the impertinence of staying away so long. They reunited in the sitting room, where Master was having tea with a dark-haired little girl that couldn’t have been more than eight years old.   
“Celeste\! So you return at last,” Master said.   
“Yes,” Celeste answered stiffly. “What is that?”  
“This,” Master gestured to the girl. “Is Marianne.”  
The little girl looked up. “Hello, Sister.”  
Celeste frowned, displeasure rising. “She is one of us? You’ve taken on another apprentice?”  
Master sipped her tea and nodded. “Don’t be so cross. You haven’t been to see me for half a century, of course I got lonely.”  
Celeste merely clicked her tongue disapprovingly.   
“But there is something else,” said Master. “I heard about you looking for a child. Marianne’s brother is just a babe, but not one of us. He might suit you.”  
“I’ve already seen many children, and none of them are to my liking. They’re all too loud and annoying,” Celeste said.   
“Then wait until you see this one.” Master gestured to a servant, who promptly left the room. A few minutes later, she returned with a bundle in her arms.   
Gently, the servant handed the bundle to Celeste.   
The baby slept peacefully, sucking on its thumb. He was a pretty little thing, with a head of dark hair. This was unfair, since sleeping babies were always cute.   
“Just wait until it wakes up,” Celeste scowled. “It’ll cry and scream until we’re all deaf.”  
“I gave it a calming drought,” Master gave an exasperated sigh and snapped her fingers. “I thought it worked rather well. See for yourself.”  
The baby’s eyes snapped open, dark yet bright. It smiled, and a knot twisted in the woman’s stomach. She glanced up sharply at her master.   
“You’ve… he…” she could not bring herself to finish.   
*You’ve made him look like the child I might have had, with him.*  
Master observed her with mild amusement and she glared back contemptuously, but she could never win against Master. Finally, she relented.   
“I will keep the child,” she announced.   
And so she did. All three of them and the servants raised the boy together, in the house by the sea. Celeste treated the boy, who she considered her son, as one might treat a treasured pet. She adored him and loved him like no other in the world, and felt proud of herself for being quite successful. Even the presence of Marianne could be tolerated, for after all she was the one that brought the bundle of energy and joy to the house, though they never became close, given the little girl’s quiet reserve.   
“Say, aren’t you glad we kept him?” Master said smugly one evening.   
“Oh, I suppose,” replied Celeste absently, smiling as she looked through the drawings the boy made for her. They were simply awful, but adorable. His name, which they had decided to be Peter, was messily scrawled in the corner.   
Marianne sat by, studying. The clock chimed and she stood as the boy ran into the room, dressed in his night clothes and holding a book. He was four now, and just adorable.   
“I’ll tuck him in,” said Marianne. Celeste raised an eyebrow and got up.   
“I can do that,” she said. “Peter likes me better.”  
The boy’s eyes widened and he grinned brightly. “Yes\! Can Mama read to me?” He hugged the woman’s legs, which were all he could reach.   
“Of course,” said Celeste. She patted the child’s head, then paused for a moment as she looked into his large and expectant dark eyes.   
He couldn’t possibly want her to carry him, could he? Well, perhaps she could do something better. Celeste flicked her hand and the boy shot up into the air.   
Peter let out a surprised yelp before he started laughing, a sweet and bubbly sound. Celeste smiled, and together they went to the boy’s room. She tucked him in bed and took the book from his hands, and began reading.   
“Once upon a time, there was a boy and a girl, who lived next to a witch in the woods…”  
Already, she could see where this was going. How amusing mortals thought they were.   
“... the witch died, and the boy and the girl lived happily ever after.”  
What a miserable waste of paper. Celeste rolled her eyes and tossed the book back onto its shelf. Peter yawned.   
“Are you a witch, Mama?” asked Peter. “Grandma and Anne too?”  
“Something like that,” said Celeste. “But we won’t die, not for a very long time.”  
“Mama, I love you\!” Peter hugged Celeste tightly. “I wanna live with you and Anne and Grandma forever\! You won’t leave me, right?”  
“Of course,” Celeste said coolly. The boy would only last a brief moment in her life. He would grow old, wither, and fade, while she remained frozen in time. But he didn’t need to know that.   
So she tucked him in tightly and kissed him on the forehead. No use thinking about the future, there would be too much time after. The love came first, and it was to be enjoyed as long as possible before the loss, like always.  
Some more years passed and suddenly the boy was eight. He was beginning to look a lot like the man Celeste pretended to be his father, but he had a different air about him, energetic to the point of annoyance. Fortunately, Marianne took care of most of that, allowing Celeste to continue to spoil him.   
“Mama\!” Peter bursted into the room, pouting. “I wanna go outside\!”  
It was sunny out, the sun reflecting off of the blinding blanket of snow. Celeste could feel that a blizzard would approach by night, though it was presently just after noon.   
“Then why don’t you?” said Celeste, not looking up from her book.   
“Anne won’t let me,” the boy looked up to his mother, his eyes wide like a puppy’s. “But can I?”  
“Oh, yes,” Celeste waved dismissively. “Take Sarah with you, and be back before dark.”  
The boy beamed. He leaned up and kissed his mother on the cheek, and his mother, feeling immensely satisfied with herself, but also wondering why he wasn’t on his way yet, smiled with a hint of irritation.   
Celeste did not think of anything else that day, for she was engrossed in a delightful magazine with all the latest hat designs. As she went to bed, she was vaguely aware that she may have forgotten something important, but she brushed it off, taking it to be a result of centuries of paranoia.   
She rose early the next day to the sound of sobs.   
“What has happened?” she demanded as she descended the stairs.   
An elderly woman wept in an armchair, her hair grayer than Celeste remembered. Marianne stood beside her stoically, trying to get the housekeeper to take a tonic. Master sat cross-legged, hovering in mid air with her eyes firmly shut. A moment later, she disappeared.  
“Sarah lost Peter yesterday,” said Marianne, after the housekeeper drank the tonic and calmed to soft sniffles. “She tried to look for him, then forgot what she was doing.”  
“Nonsense,” said Celeste. “She’s only fifty, the last time I checked.”  
“And when might that have been?”  
“Only about twenty years ago, and I don’t see how that’s relevant.” Celeste looked out the window, to the snowstorm that raged on. “How come you didn’t realize?”  
“I was out with Master,” Marianne said quietly. “We only got back this morning, and Sarah remembered when she saw us.”  
Celeste studied the old woman afresh. Wrinkles covered her loosened skin, and her eyes drooped shut. She began to snore softly. It seemed like yesterday when she was just a young and pretty maiden looking for work, unable to ever fall victim to the cruel hands of time. But she, like the many others before her, would soon leave, and leave nothing behind except fading memories in the immortal minds of few and a familiar emptiness.   
It was different for Peter. He was so little, he had so many years before him. She would raise him and cherish him, and he would accompany her until she found someone else to take his place after. Her joy, her treasure, her son.   
Master came back through the front door, a small bundle in her arms. His skin was stiff and pale, dried blood and darkened bruises still visible as Master laid him down.   
“The boys from town found him,” Master muttered angrily. “Said he lived with witches, so he must be a witch. Beat him up and left him for dead.”  
Peter had not been this motionless since he learned how to walk. It was unnatural, wrong, in the most horrible ways, a dark pit swallowing them both. Celeste touched his hand. He was so cold.   
Celeste couldn’t remember what happened after that.

Dead.   
All dead. Long gone.   
Nothing left.   
The woman rose, silent tears streaking down her face. At least, back then, she still had Sarah and Master. At least, she hadn’t… ah, no matter now.   
Moonlight streamed through the windows in the hall. She wandered through the house aimlessly until she found herself in front of the same door she had approached on many sleepless nights, once upon a time.   
She smiled bitterly and slipped inside.   
What had she expected? The woman she longed to see, working at her desk, as if nothing ever happened? For her to look up with her wry gaze, and ask what was wrong? To be commended on some new discovery she made?  
There was no familiar lantern nor greeting, only the lonely gleam of the moon falling onto dust-covered scrolls. The woman sat in the chair her master used to occupy. Though the house was hers now, it felt wrong, it was wrong. She laid her head on the table, and sobbed.

Forests, mountains, rivers, cities. Skies, swamps, lakes, villages. It took one to love one; it took one to kill one. They found you, wherever you hid. To the end of the world they would hunt you down, and they had more than enough time.   
A young woman stood at the front of a store, a suitcase beside her. The owner, a middle-aged matron, stepped out and embraced the young woman.   
“Oh, Jennifer, we’ll miss you very much,” said the matron. “Do you really have to go?”  
“I’m afraid yes, ma’am,” said the young woman, Jennifer. “I have to take care of my mother, her health is getting worse now she’s older.”  
“Ah, don’t we all,” the matron sighed and smiled sadly. She patted Jennifer’s cheek. “But look at you, you haven’t aged a day. You must’ve worked here more than a decade, no?”  
“Yes, ma’am. Thirteen years.”  
“Well, do take care,” said the woman. “And remember not to take that road by the sea. It’s cursed, everyone says, from so many years ago.”  
“Yes, ma’am. Thank you very much.”  
The one that called herself Jennifer kissed the woman on the cheek and turned to leave. She remembered not to go by the sea, but she also did not go by the road. Into the woods she went, and with a snap of her fingers her appearance shifted back into her own.   
The fools had not noticed her, waltzing right underneath their eyes. So long it had been the perfect sanctuary, for no one could have imagined that she would dare return so close, and now she laughed in their faces. Yet regardless, the prospect of an unwelcome reunion was driving her away, much to her displeasure.   
A presence followed behind. She had come.   
The one forced to have different names fled. The pursuer chased.   
It had been many years, too many and not enough. That woman was old, she shouldn’t be able to be this fast. Compared, the escapee was merely a little girl, the way she may always appear to the one that raised her.   
In the end, the prey could not defeat the predator. The student could not surpass the teacher. The chase was pointless. Or had it every meaning in the world?  
The two women stopped in a clearing. The elder spoke first.   
“It’s been a while, Celeste.”  
“Master.”  
They looked at each other, and Celeste knew. No blame, no distrust, no hatred. She understood, she knew, she did not have to forgive because there was no crime. Celeste flung her arms around her only family in the world and laughed and sobbed and wept.   
“My girl,” Master whispered. “Oh, Celeste.”  
When the girl composed herself, Master held her hands tightly. Her skin was soft and wrinkled, though she did not appear to have aged.   
“A master is not made to hunt her apprentice for a crime like this,” said Master. “You ought to have sought me out.”  
“I’m sorry,” said Celeste. “They might have been watching. I’ll be burned at the stake.”  
“That is why I’m here,” said Master. She squeezed her hands. “The Guild’s agreed to reduce your sentence to a millennia of confinement. Nine centuries if you turn yourself in.”  
Celeste narrowed her eyes. “It wasn’t my fault. You know that.”  
“Of course,” said Master. “But a town was still annihilated, and to this day people think it was cursed. You’re strong, and there were traces, even after the Guild wiped as much as they could.”  
“You know why I did it. They deserved every bit.”  
“You weren’t yourself.”  
Celeste shook her head. “I tore them to bits, I wanted them to suffer as much as their mortal bodies would allow, because they would never understand how much I did. I would have done the same today.”  
Suddenly, she tensed. A presence was approaching, and Celeste snatched her hands away from Master.  
“Marianne’s coming,” she growled. “I know she works for the Guild now. You must be so proud.”  
“I’m trying to do what’s best for you,” Master said gently. “You can’t run forever, you know.”  
“They haven’t caught me yet, have they?” Celeste backed away. “But you betrayed me.”  
“You disobeyed me first,” Master’s voice hardened. “The very first thing I taught you was not to harm mortals.”  
“Their lives are worthless, short as they are,” Celeste retorted. “Yet they dared to cause me pain that I shall have to bear to the end of time.”  
“You do not understand,” Master hissed. “Have you forgotten everything? What about Peter? It is because their lives are short that they are more valuable.”  
“They harmed their own.” Anger swelled in her chest. “They killed my Peter. Their lives were forfeit by nature, even if their laws did not dictate it.”  
“But not the lives of their townspeople,” Master snapped. “You should’ve known better. Marianne would’ve.”  
“Then go and hand me to her\!” Celeste screamed. “She hunts her own sister, for a crime not committed. She’s fooled you, she’s wicked, just like the mortals\!”  
“If that’s what you think, then fine,” Master snarled. “You are no apprentice of mine.”  
“Enough\!”  
Celeste threw her hands forward. The air froze for a split moment, as if for eternity, then ignited. The inferno burst from everywhere, all at once, consuming everything in its path. Master was thrown back, but Celeste rushed to her side, ignoring the scorching heat.   
The elderly woman was covered in flames that devoured her. Her veins blackened, her body convulsed violently, and she opened her mouth in silent cries. She stretched out a hand, blackened and twitching, a finger that pointed at Celeste, before she fell lifeless, her face older than it should’ve been possible, her body ablaze. The black of her veins seeped away, back wherever they came from, leaving the woman’s body to smolder and burn like any corpse.  
“No, no…\!” Celeste wailed. The heat refused to devour her as she so desperately wished it would in that moment, her instinctual abilities keeping them at bay.   
She couldn’t stay here for long, the flames would swallow her too. Perhaps she desired that, to finally rest in everlasting peace, but she could not, would not. Already Master’s body was alight, so dead and powerless, so mortal, the picture of her worst fear.   
Marianne was coming. If she stayed, death approached, and that wouldn’t do. She had started over so many times before, she could do so again, except this time without the only one constant in her existence.   
With one last glance to the inferno, and the woman within that once loved her as her own, Celeste fled.

For years, they said the place that used to be a town was haunted by a woman’s agonized screams for her son; and the charred forest that once stood tall was filled with her shrieking and weeping for the one who was to her a mother.   
The woman was tired. Though she may not look it, she had lived a long life.   
The soft music of piano sounded through the house. She knew then, that she was dreaming. Nevertheless, she followed it into the parlour, out the door, onto the beach.   
A man played in the distance, completely engrossed in his music. She dared not disturb him, but she was content, to see him once more, so passionate and alive.   
‘Mama.’   
A child laughed, and a small figure appeared in the distance, on the sea. He played with the water, carefree and happy as always. The woman let out a strangled sob.   
‘Celeste.’  
An elderly woman, her silhouette so clear and real under the bright moonlight. She stood on the water, her arms wide open with a gentle smile.   
The woman, the witch, the lover, the mother, the student, the daughter.  
Celeste.   
She broke into a run and flung herself into Master’s arms, tears streaming down her face.   
Behind her, the house was ablaze. She did not know, did not care, that her body still lay in bed, burning. The one that once called her sister stood in the shadows as the house with so many memories was consumed by flames, along with its last mistress.   
And that was the end of it all, Celeste and the ones she loved, the ones she lost.
